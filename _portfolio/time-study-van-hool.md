Van Hool, the Belgian vehicle manufacturer, opened one of its production sites to a *Methods Engineering and Work Measurement* class, and the assignment was to do what a shop floor engineer would do with a stopwatch and a clipboard: find out how a workstation really works, and make it work better.

The project had three parts:

1. **Methods study.** A critical look at the existing workplace, and a reasoned argument for which technique the work should use.
2. **Time study.** A **direct time study** to establish a standard time, plus a **work sampling** study as a cross-check, with a discussion of the accuracy each method can support.
3. **The future state.** Proposed and quantified improvements, delivered as a proper **optimized standard work method**: an operative instruction sheet with a well-grounded standard time, not a set of suggestions.

Two site visits did most of the work, one to learn the process and one for the tablet-based measurements, with presentations in between and a written report at the end.

Two things stick with me about industrial engineering in a company like this. First, the observed method and the *intended* method are rarely the same thing, and the gap between them is where most of the available time is hiding. Second, a standard time you cannot defend is worse than no standard time at all: the whole point of doing the study properly (allowances, work sampling, accuracy discussion) is that the number survives being challenged by the person who has to meet it.
